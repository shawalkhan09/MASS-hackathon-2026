### 1. Root Cause Analysis (5 Whys)

*   **Problem:** Peloton held ~$1.05 billion in unsold inventory by early 2023.
*   **Why #1:** Production volume significantly exceeded actual consumer demand throughout 2021 and early 2022.
*   **Why #2:** Peloton’s manufacturing capacity was aggressively scaled up via the $400M Precor acquisition and the $400M Ohio factory project.
*   **Why #3:** Leadership extrapolated the pandemic-driven 300% demand surge as a permanent, long-term shift in the addressable market.
*   **Why #4:** The company lacked a flexible, variable-cost supply chain model, opting instead for capital-intensive, fixed-asset ownership.
*   **Why #5 (Root Cause):** **Strategic Failure of Governance:** Management lacked a mechanism to distinguish temporary, exogenous demand shocks (COVID lockdowns) from structural market growth, leading to an over-commitment of capital toward fixed manufacturing capacity that could not be retracted once demand normalized.

### 3. Pareto Analysis (Impact Prioritization)

The crisis impact is driven by two "vital few" factors that account for the overwhelming majority of the financial damage ($1.05B in inventory + $439M net loss):

1.  **Demand Forecasting/Extrapolation Error (~70% of impact):** This caused the decision to over-produce and over-invest. Without this, the inventory would not have reached the $1B+ level.
2.  **Fixed-Asset Supply Chain Rigidity (~25% of impact):** Because the investment was in *fixed* assets (factory/Precor) rather than *variable* ones (outsourcing), Peloton could not pivot when forecasts proved wrong.
3.  **Compounding Factors (~5% of impact):** The Tread+ safety recall and associated brand damage represent a significant, but non-systemic, drag on the business.

*Note: Precise percentages are estimates based on the scale of the inventory glut versus the scope of the recall; the data provided confirms the recall was a "compounding factor" rather than a primary driver.*

### 4. Break-even & Financial Sensitivity

The $800M capital commitment (Precor + Ohio Factory) fundamentally shifted Peloton’s break-even point.

*   **Financial Constraint:** Peloton’s business model was historically lean. By transitioning to a fixed-asset model, they increased their **Operating Leverage**.
*   **The Math:** 
    *   **Fixed Costs (FC):** Increased significantly due to the $800M capital expenditure and associated depreciation/maintenance/operating costs. 
    *   **Contribution Margin (CM):** Per unit profit.
    *   **Break-even Point (Units) = Fixed Costs / CM per unit.**
*   **Analysis:** As fixed costs skyrocketed, the volume of units required to reach the break-even point increased proportionally. When demand reverted to pre-pandemic levels (a ~300% drop from the surge peak), the number of units sold fell below this new, much higher break-even threshold. The company shifted from a profitable, variable-cost model to a loss-making, fixed-cost model, resulting in the $439 million FY2022 net loss. 
*   **Data Limitation:** The case does not provide the specific unit contribution margin or the exact monthly operating expenses of the new facilities, so an exact unit-break-even volume cannot be calculated; however, the directional logic is clear: the high fixed-asset investment necessitated a volume of sales that the post-pandemic market could no longer sustain.

### Final Root-Cause Summary
The triggering event was the **sharp reversal of consumer demand** in late 2021 as pandemic lockdowns ended. However, the **root cause** was the failure of leadership to maintain a variable-cost supply chain, instead electing to sink $800M into fixed, owned manufacturing capacity based on the flawed assumption that a temporary, pandemic-driven demand spike represented a permanent expansion of their total addressable market.
