### 1. Root Cause Analysis (5 Whys)
*Focusing on the $1.05 billion inventory collapse:*

1.  **Why did Peloton end up with $1.05 billion in unsold inventory?**
    *   *Answer:* The company produced units based on forecasts from October 2021 that drastically overestimated consumer demand for 2022.
2.  **Why did the October 2021 forecasts overestimate demand?**
    *   *Answer:* Leadership extrapolated the 300% pandemic-driven demand surge as a permanent shift in market size rather than a temporary "pull-forward" of future sales.
3.  **Why did management commit to production levels based on that extrapolation?**
    *   *Answer:* They were locked into fixed, high-volume manufacturing strategies, including the $400M Precor acquisition and the $400M Ohio factory, which required high utilization rates to justify their ROI.
4.  **Why was the company forced into this high-risk, fixed-asset manufacturing strategy?**
    *   *Answer:* They prioritized vertical integration and owned-capacity to solve the 2021 supply chain bottlenecks ($100M+ in expedited freight) without establishing a variable-cost "cushion" (e.g., third-party contract manufacturing).
5.  **Why was the supply chain designed to be so rigid?**
    *   *Answer (Root Cause):* Leadership failed to implement a flexible, agile supply chain strategy, choosing capital-intensive ownership to solve a short-term fulfillment crisis, effectively betting the entire company's operational structure on the permanence of a temporary, external anomaly.

### 2. Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram
*Mapping the factors contributing to the inventory collapse:*

*   **Process (Strategy/Planning):**
    *   Forecast failure: Misinterpreting temporary pandemic-era "pull-forward" demand as a new baseline.
    *   Inflexible supply chain: Heavy reliance on owned-manufacturing rather than a hybrid/variable-cost model.
*   **Machine (Capital Infrastructure):**
    *   $400M investment in "Peloton Output Park" (Ohio).
    *   $400M acquisition of Precor.
    *   *Impact:* Created massive fixed overhead that necessitated high output regardless of actual market sentiment.
*   **Measurement (Data/Forecasting):**
    *   October 2021 projections were fundamentally disconnected from the slowing trend of pandemic-era growth.
*   **Environment (External Factors):**
    *   COVID-19 normalization: Gyms reopening and outdoor activity replacing home workouts.

### 3. Decision Tree Analysis (Retrospective Evaluation)
*Analyzing the logic of the $400M Ohio Factory investment:*

*   **Decision Node:** Build vs. Outsource manufacturing capacity (mid-2021).
*   **Path A (Build/Owned Factory - $400M):**
    *   *Outcome 1 (Demand stays high):* High margins, inventory availability, high asset utilization.
    *   *Outcome 2 (Demand normalizes):* Excess inventory, idle capacity, $439M+ annual loss, stock price collapse.
*   **Path B (Third-Party/Contract Manufacturing):**
    *   *Outcome 1 (Demand stays high):* Lower margins (fees to contractors), supply constraints.
    *   *Outcome 2 (Demand normalizes):* Cancel orders, minimal inventory holding costs, no $400M sunken asset.

**Analytical Conclusion:** The "Build" decision relied on an implicit, untested assumption of >90% probability for Outcome 1. Management failed to perform a weighted expected-value calculation. If the probability of demand normalization was modeled at even 30-40%, the expected value of the $400M investment would have been negative, as the cost of liquidating excess inventory and carrying fixed overhead far outweighs the cost of missed sales in a scalable (outsourced) model.

### Final Root-Cause Statement
The triggering event for the inventory collapse was the post-pandemic normalization of consumer exercise habits. However, the **root cause** was a **strategic failure in risk management**: leadership incorrectly treated a temporary, external surge in demand as a permanent expansion of the addressable market, then deployed a capital-intensive, fixed-asset strategy to serve that false projection. By locking capital into owned manufacturing (Precor/Ohio) to solve a short-term supply bottleneck, they removed the operational agility required to pivot when the market inevitably corrected.
